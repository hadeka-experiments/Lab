Screen Camera

Here the camera is covered and sees no image. It only senses whether total light goes up or down, like a closed eye sensing the sun. The screen lights the paper in front of it with hundreds of black‑and‑white patterns in a row, and from how the total light changes with each pattern, an image of the paper is reconstructed. This method is called a single‑pixel camera.

Set up the experiment
  1. Turn off the room lights. It must be as dark as possible.
  2. On white paper, draw a large, bold shape with a thick black marker, like an X or a circle, filling most of the page.
  3. Cover the front camera with a folded piece of tissue or thin paper taped over it, so the camera can’t see the drawing. It only needs to sense light.
  4. Lay the phone on its back, screen up, put two matchboxes or anything 2–4 cm tall at either end, and rest the paper on them with the drawing facing down toward the screen.

Step 1 is optional but helps: without the paper, with the screen facing empty space or the ceiling, the page records the light leaking from the screen straight into the camera and subtracts it later.

How does an image appear without a camera?
  • The screen is divided into squares, and each pattern lights half of them and darkens the other half in a special order called Hadamard.
  • Squares facing a white part of the paper reflect more light than those facing black ink. So each pattern gives one number: how much light came back.
  • Every pattern is followed immediately by its inverse, and the difference cancels room light and camera drift. From hundreds of these numbers, an equation is solved that produces an image.
  • The image is blurry, because each screen square lights a larger area of the paper. The closer the paper, the sharper the image.
  • The image is from the screen’s point of view, so you may need to flip it left/right to match the paper.
  • ⚠ This is the hardest experiment. If the camera adjusts its exposure midway, or the phone moves, the image will be noisy.
