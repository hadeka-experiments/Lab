Sky Darkness

The phone looks at the night sky, stacks dozens of frames so faint stars show up, and counts the stars it sees. The less city light, the more stars. Compare your rooftop with the countryside or the desert.

  1. Go somewhere open, away from any nearby lamp.
  2. Enter the place name and tap “Start”.
  3. For the first 3 seconds cover the camera with your finger: this teaches the page which sensor pixels glow falsely.
  4. Then lay the phone face down on the ground or a wall, so the camera looks straight up, anddon’t touch it for 15 seconds.

Comparison

    How does it count, and what affects the number?
    • The page stacks about 90 camera frames. Random noise cancels out as they add up, while stars stay in place and get stronger.
    • It removes the sky glow (which varies slowly across the image) and looks for small points at least 6× brighter than the noise around them. Points that glowed while the camera was covered are discarded.
    • The number is relative: a web page can’t fix the camera’s exposure time; the camera sets it automatically. So the number only compares fairly on the same phone, in full darkness (at least 1.5 hours after sunset).
    • The moon and clouds reduce stars a lot. Note whether the moon is up so comparisons are fair.
    • The phone camera in video mode isn’t like night‑photo mode, so it only catches the brighter stars. In a big city the count may be zero or very low, which is itself a measure of light pollution.
